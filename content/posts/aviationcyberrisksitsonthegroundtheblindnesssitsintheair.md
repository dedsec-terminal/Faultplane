Here are three concise paragraphs summarizing the article:

The aviation industry's cyber risk is often misunderstood, with many focusing on the possibility of "hacking a plane" while neglecting the more significant risks on the ground. According to Eliran Almog, CEO of Cyviation, almost all material cyber losses in the aviation industry occur on the ground, including reservations, ground handling, and airport operations. In contrast, no aircraft has caused a significant cyber loss for a carrier yet. However, the aircraft is still a critical component, as it consumes data from the ground constantly, making it an endpoint of a supply chain that is often unmonitored.

Almog highlights the importance of understanding attacker behavior in aviation environments, citing examples such as GNSS interference, which can cause false position fixes and inertial system degradation without leaving any logs or alerts. He also notes that many systems in the aviation ecosystem, such as ACARS and ADS-B, were built without authentication, making them vulnerable to exploitation. Additionally, Almog emphasizes the need for digital twins, which can model an aircraft's subsystems and communication paths, allowing for testing and simulation of cyber attacks without compromising the actual aircraft.

For a carrier with limited resources, such as 30 aircraft and only two people in IT security, a credible security program requires ruthless prioritization. This includes inventorying dependencies, securing the ground infrastructure, and purchasing aircraft visibility solutions. Almog advises against pursuing bespoke threat-intel programs or frameworks for their own sake, instead focusing on building a solid foundation of security basics, such as multi-factor authentication and backups. He also emphasizes the importance of incident response planning, as the difference between a bad week and a disaster often depends on whether someone has thought through the first few hours ahead of time.
