Here are three concise paragraphs summarizing the article and comments:

US Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) is buying access to credit card records through data brokers, allowing them to obtain information provided by individuals when opening a credit card. This data pipeline, which includes banks, credit bureaus, and Thomson Reuters' CLEAR platform, does not require a warrant. The data is being used to support ICE's mission, including the identification of voter fraud, immigration fraud, and national security threats.

Commenters have expressed concern about the lack of transparency and oversight in the data broker market, with some calling for legislation such as The Fourth Amendment Is Not For Sale Act. Others have noted that the use of data brokers is not unique to ICE, with companies like Apple, Alphabet, and Microsoft also collecting and using personal data. The discussion has also touched on the history of government surveillance and the potential for abuse of power, with some drawing parallels to past authoritarian regimes.

The conversation has also highlighted the complexities of managing personal data and the trade-offs between convenience, security, and privacy. As one commenter noted, "if you want to realistically participate in the financial system, you basically have to give up" to data collection. Others have pointed out that the use of data brokers and surveillance technologies is not limited to government agencies, but is also used by corporations and other organizations. The debate raises important questions about the balance between individual rights and national security, and the need for greater transparency and accountability in the use of personal data.
