Here are three concise paragraphs summarizing the article:

The EU AI Act's first year of enforcement is expected to be a "bedding in" period, with corrective orders likely outnumbering large fines. Edwin Weijdema, Field CTO at Veeam, notes that regulators will consider proportionality, scale of impact, and cooperation when making decisions. While fines of up to 15 million euros or 3% of worldwide turnover are possible, the bigger practical exposure may be operational, with organizations potentially being ordered to suspend or withdraw AI-enabled processes.

The EU AI Act's transparency obligations apply when a person interacts with an AI system, unless it's obvious that they're dealing with AI. Weijdema explains that the channel of interaction is not decisive, and companies need to make deliberate choices to separate internal and customer-facing agents. Security teams running simulated phishing exercises using cloned voices or AI-generated content must carefully assess the legal basis and risk of their decisions and document their reasoning. They should consider alternatives, such as using fictional personas or synthetic voices that don't imitate real employees.

The first Article 50 action is likely to come from a regulator, but may be triggered by a complaint from a consumer group, competitor, or individual. Weijdema notes that clients are asking how to prove what an AI agent did, why it did it, and who was accountable, but this remains a hard question with no good answer yet. He advises treating AI agents like privileged digital identities, with owners, defined roles, and monitoring. Other unanswered questions include who is ultimately accountable when an AI system causes harm, how to prove AI governance is working in practice, and how much business value is willing to be lost to stay compliant and transparent when using AI at scale.
