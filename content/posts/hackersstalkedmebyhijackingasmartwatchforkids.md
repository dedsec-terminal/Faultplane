Here are three concise paragraphs summarizing the article:

A security researcher, Vangelis Stykas, demonstrated how he could hack a children's smartwatch, tracking the wearer's location, taking photos, and listening to audio without the wearer's knowledge. The watch, which cost less than $30, was made by an obscure company called CJC and manufactured by YiQingTeng Electronics in Shenzhen, China. Stykas was able to exploit the watch's vulnerabilities, which included a lack of authentication, to gain access to its features and track the wearer's movements.

The researchers found that the vulnerabilities in the watch were not isolated, but rather part of a larger problem with GPS-enabled devices made by various companies in Shenzhen, China. They discovered that over 30 brands of smartwatches and car accessories used the same technology and backend servers as the hacked watch, making them potentially vulnerable to the same forms of digital stalking. The researchers also found that two other major GPS platforms, SinoTrack and NewGPS2012, had significant security flaws, including SQL injection vulnerabilities and lack of authentication, which could allow hackers to access and control devices.

The researchers have been warning the companies behind these platforms about the vulnerabilities for months, but many of the issues remain unaddressed. The sheer number of devices affected and the lack of transparency about the technology and backend servers used by these companies make it difficult for consumers to know which devices are secure. The researchers are calling attention to the problem, highlighting the need for better security and regulation in the industry to protect consumers, particularly children, from the risks of hacking and tracking. They will present their findings at the Black Hat cybersecurity conference, hoping to raise awareness and prompt action to address the vulnerabilities.
