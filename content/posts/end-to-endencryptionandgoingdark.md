Here is a 3-paragraph summary of the article and comments:

The article discusses the ongoing debate over end-to-end encryption (E2EE) and the concept of "going dark," where law enforcement and national security agencies are unable to access encrypted communications. The authors analyze the current controversy over E2EE, which they call "Round 3" of the Going Dark Debate, and explain the underlying technologies and market developments. They identify five technically distinct scenarios for how E2EE operates in practice, each with different implications for lawful access, and argue that E2EE is not limited to messaging but is embedded throughout the modern technology stack.

The article concludes that any law broadly limiting E2EE would have severe consequences for cybersecurity, commerce, and government operations. The authors also note that the lessons from previous rounds of the debate, including the "least trusted country problem" and the "golden age of surveillance," remain relevant today. They argue that new government claims for restricting effective encryption deserve skepticism and that the technical decision on E2EE has been fully decided, with the proof being that "going dark" cannot be stopped.

Commenter Clive Robinson agrees that the battle for E2EE is effectively over, citing the work of Claude Shannon and Gus Simmons, as well as a new paper from NIST. He argues that the next battle will be over "user content" via "client-side scanning" carried out by AI, but notes that the technical findings on E2EE are transferrable to this issue as well. Robinson concludes that the authoritarians will not give up on the idea of restricting encryption and will continue to fight, despite having lost the war.
