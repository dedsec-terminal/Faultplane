Here are three concise paragraphs summarizing the article:

The US Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) collected nearly 1 million people's DNA in 2025, including those of young children, as part of a sweeping expansion of DNA collection from people held for civil immigration violations. This campaign, backed by the threat of prosecution, funnels genetic profiles into the FBI's Combined DNA Index System (CODIS), a database built for criminal investigations. The majority of people in ICE custody have no criminal conviction, and residing in the US undocumented is typically a civil, not criminal, offense.

The expansion of DNA collection has sparked lawsuits and drawn congressional scrutiny, with lawmakers expressing concern that children are being swabbed at family detention centers. A joint statement from US representatives Joaquin Castro, Greg Stanton, and Nanette Barragán noted that families at the Dilley, Texas, detention center have not been convicted of a crime and do not belong in a database meant for violent criminals. In response to questions, a DHS spokesperson defended DNA collection as a border-security and identification measure, but did not address estimates that ICE may have added hundreds of thousands of profiles to CODIS in 2025.

The collection of DNA from immigration detainees has been criticized by Georgetown Law's Center on Privacy and Technology, which estimates that DHS has become the largest single source of new genetic profiles in the nation's criminal DNA system. The center argues that the primary purpose of the DNA-collection authority is to build a national DNA database for solving crimes, rather than to establish identity or confirm family relationships. This has raised concerns about the Fourth Amendment and the government's ability to compel individuals to hand over sensitive information without suspicion. The issue has also led to prosecutions, including the case of Hugo Moreno-Mendez, who was found guilty of refusing to provide a DNA sample and sentenced to time served.
