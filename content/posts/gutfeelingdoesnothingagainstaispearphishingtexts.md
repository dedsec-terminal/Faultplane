Here are three concise paragraphs summarizing the article:

A recent study at Brigham Young University tested the effectiveness of AI-generated spear phishing texts against human-written ones. Twenty-five volunteers were given 12 text messages each, six generated by GPT-4 and six written by students, and asked to sort them from most to least likely to get a click. The results showed that the AI-generated messages were slightly more effective, but the difference was not statistically significant. The AI messages landed above the "click line" 28% of the time, while the human-written ones managed 21.3%.

The study found that work-related messages were the most effective, clearing the click line 38% of the time, while hobby-themed messages managed 19% and social media-themed messages managed 17%. The use of personal details in messages was also found to be effective, but only if the details were accurate. Incorrect details were found to be worse than none at all, as they gave the recipient a reason to be suspicious. The study also found that participants were unable to reliably distinguish between AI-generated and human-written messages, with only 52% of judgments correct, which is no better than chance.

The study's results have implications for phishing detection and prevention. The researchers found that a machine learning classifier was able to distinguish between AI-generated and human-written messages with 88.7% accuracy, but this classifier was trained on a specific dataset and may not generalize to other contexts. The study's authors conclude that the best way to defend against phishing attacks is to check the sender, channel, link, and request against what you would expect to receive, rather than trying to decide whether the message sounds like it was written by a human or a robot. This approach is more reliable and effective than relying on instinct or trying to detect AI-generated messages.
