Here are three concise paragraphs summarizing the article:

Researchers from the University of California at San Diego and Oberlin College have developed a coin-sized device that can hack into a Boeing 737's autopilot system, potentially allowing an attacker to redirect the plane's navigation or alter critical flight calculations. The device, which costs less than $100, can be fitted into a port on the exterior of the plane in under a minute, and can send electrical signals to spoof commands to the plane's computer systems. This could lead to subtle changes in the plane's behavior, such as runway overruns or diversions into another country's airspace.

The researchers, who have been working on the project for over a decade, have tested their device on a custom-built avionics test bed and have demonstrated its ability to intercept, alter, and spoof commands to the plane's sensitive systems. They have shared their findings with Boeing, which has downplayed the practical risk of the attack, citing the layers of protection in place on the airplane. However, the researchers argue that their technique represents a significant blind spot in aircraft security and could be exploited by well-resourced saboteurs.

The researchers are not revealing the details of their attack, but they are calling for long-term changes in the cybersecurity of airplane components and operational security measures to prevent such attacks. They suggest simple fixes, such as plugging the vulnerable port with epoxy or removing it altogether. The team's work has implications for the aviation industry, and they hope to raise awareness about the potential risks of physical access hacking. While they do not recommend grounding aircraft, they believe that the industry should take their findings seriously and plan to defend against such attacks.
